The guest list is the first document an Armenian wedding actually starts with, well before the venue or the dress get chosen. It's also, almost without exception, the most contentious part of the whole planning process — not because the couple can't count, but because the list is never theirs alone.

## Why the list grows on its own

At a Western wedding, the couple invites individual guests. At an Armenian one, families are invited, and deciding who belongs to that family isn't a decision the couple makes by themselves. Mom has coworkers she's worked alongside for twenty years. Dad has business partners and childhood friends. Grandma has neighbors she's known since her own children were born. Every one of these people counts as "family" in the local sense, and leaving them off reads as a deliberate snub to an entire branch of relatives.

That's where the numbers come from: 250–400 guests in Yerevan isn't extravagant, it's the average wedding size. A guest list of 120 is closer to the exception than the rule here, and usually signals either a tight budget or a deliberate choice by the couple to go small against everyone's expectations.

## Who actually writes the list

It helps to accept early on that the list is written by at least three parties: the couple and both sets of parents. Each side has its own list, and the couple's real job isn't to dictate numbers — it's to merge three separate lists into one without alienating anyone.

A simple structure works well. Agree in advance on the total number of seats — say, a venue that seats 320. Split that quota into roughly three equal shares: the couple's own guests, the groom's side, the bride's side. Each side then manages its own third independently. This defuses half the potential conflict, since parents get a clear ceiling instead of feeling cut at the last minute.

## Categories you can barely trim

Before arguing over numbers, it's worth acknowledging that some guests aren't up for debate.

- **Godparents and their families.** The kavor and kavorkin hold a special role at an Armenian wedding, from the church ceremony to the toasts at the table. Leaving them or their close relatives off the list is considered a serious break with tradition.
- **Direct relatives up to a certain degree.** First cousins are usually automatic; second and third cousins are already up for negotiation.
- **Anyone who invited you.** If your parents attended a wedding or a funeral in another family, a reciprocal invitation isn't a courtesy — it's an obligation.

Subtract these categories from your total seat count first, and only divide what's left between "would be nice" and "maybe."

## How to talk to parents about the numbers

The most common mistake is putting off the conversation about guest count until after parents already have a finished list on paper. Talking someone into crossing names off a list that's already written is far harder than agreeing on a cap beforehand.

Start the conversation not with "who should we cut," but with venue and budget numbers instead: "We're looking at venues for 300 people, which means each side gets roughly 90–100 seats — could you work within that?" A concrete number is much easier to accept than a vague request to "trim the list."

> If the budget is tight, it's more honest to say so directly than to quietly cut a parent's list after they've already finalized it within their own family.

## What to do if the list still doesn't fit

If the sum of all three lists exceeds what any suitable venue can hold, there are working options beyond simply cutting people.

First, reconsider the neighborhood and venue format — a venue on the outskirts or just outside the city often seats 20–30% more people for the same money as a hall in central Kentron.

Second, split the celebration into two formats: the church ceremony and an intimate reception for close family on the wedding day itself, with a broader circle of relatives and acquaintances invited to a separate tea or lunch on another day. This happens more often than you'd think, especially when budget and venue capacity are fixed.

Third, agree on a hard cutoff date for the list — for example, closing it four months before the wedding, after which no side adds new names. Without that boundary, the list tends to keep growing right up until the seating chart is printed.

## Seating and final numbers

The final list isn't just for invitations — it drives seating arrangements, the menu, and the venue budget, which is usually calculated per guest. When planning seating, build in a 5–8% buffer for last-minute cancellations, and confirm with the venue how the minimum guaranteed sum is calculated: most Yerevan venues charge for the agreed headcount regardless of actual turnout.

For a large guest list, it's worth discussing the final seating chart with your toastmaster (tamada) in advance — they need to know which branch of the family is sitting where, so toasts are directed correctly and no elder is left out.

## The bottom line

The guest list at an Armenian wedding isn't a list of who you'd like to invite — it's the outcome of negotiations between three families within a fixed budget. The earlier the boundaries and the rules for building the list are set, the fewer hurt feelings there'll be at the finish line, and the less chance a relative finds out a week before the wedding that they were "forgotten."
