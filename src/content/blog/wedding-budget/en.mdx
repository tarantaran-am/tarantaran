An average wedding in Yerevan isn't a hundred-guest dinner with a DJ — it's an event for 300 to 400 people, a church wedding, a tamada (toastmaster), live music, and at least two dresses for the bride. The budget grows not because anyone is bad at math, but because the whole shape of the event is different. The money conversation shouldn't start with a dream list — it should start with the guest list and a set of priorities.

## What the budget is made of

For a large Armenian wedding, spending typically breaks down like this:

- **Venue and banquet** — 45–55% of the budget. This covers the rental, the menu, and service, and it's almost always the biggest line item.
- **Photo and video** — 8–12%. With 300+ guests and a church ceremony, this is a full-day job, often requiring two photographers.
- **Decor and florals** — 8–10%. The head table, table settings, the wedding arch, the car.
- **Tamada and musicians** — 8–12%. At an Armenian wedding this isn't background noise — it's the full direction of the evening, with dances, games, and live sound.
- **Dress, suit, styling** — 6–8%.
- **Church, transport, invitations, and everything else** — the remaining 10–15%.

These percentages are a guide, not a rule. Skip live musicians and hire just an MC instead of a full tamada, and the decor and photo shares naturally grow.

## Booking order: what to lock in first

The logic is simple: secure whatever can't be postponed or swapped out at the last minute.

1. **Date and venue.** Good venues for June through September get booked out 10–12 months ahead.
2. **The church for the wedding ceremony.** Popular Yerevan churches also fill up early, especially Sunday slots during peak season.
3. **Tamada and musicians** — the best hosts and bands take one wedding a day, and their calendars close faster than venues'.
4. **Photographer.**
5. Everything else — decor, dress, invitations — can be sorted out 3–6 months out.

If the budget is tight, cut in this same order: trim decor before you trim the venue or the tamada, since those two shape the whole evening's impression.

## Where people cut corners they shouldn't

**Sound equipment.** A wedding with 300 guests dancing until dawn needs proper audio gear and a technician running it all night, not a speaker someone happened to have. Bad sound is obvious to everyone immediately, and there's no fixing it after the fact.

**Photographer and videographer.** Cutting here rarely means "cheaper photos" — it usually means skipping the second photographer at a 300+ guest wedding, which means half the toasts, the dances, and the parents' dance simply never get captured. A year later, the photos are all that's left of the evening, and almost nobody regrets what they spent on them — plenty regret what they saved.

**Menu and drinks sized to the real guest count.** Underestimating portions to fit the budget is a bad idea: at Armenian weddings guests often bring relatives along, and the venue will usually blame the discrepancy on the couple, not absorb it.

**Air conditioning and venue capacity.** A July wedding crammed into a tight hall with weak air conditioning isn't a budget win — it's a ruined evening for three hundred people.

## Where you can save without losing anything

**A weekday or off-season date.** A Thursday wedding, or one in March or April, typically costs 15–25% less than a Saturday at the peak of the season, with no drop in venue or vendor quality.

**The second bridal look.** A beautiful but less expensive dress for the second half of the evening looks just as good and frees up a meaningful chunk of budget.

**Invitations and small decor touches.** Digital invitations instead of printed ones, a simpler guest-table setup instead of an elaborate installation — guests barely notice, but the difference on the invoice is real.

**Transport.** One nice car for the couple instead of a whole motorcade for the wedding party is money that's easier spent on the venue or the photographer.

**Out-of-season flowers.** Peonies in December cost several times what they do in May. If your date falls in winter, it's worth discussing seasonal alternatives with your decorator — visually the difference is minimal.

## Who traditionally pays for what

In the classical arrangement, the groom's side covers the major expenses — venue, banquet, musicians, the bridal car, and often the rings. The bride's side traditionally handles the dowry and the dress, and in some families, part of the decor or the photographer.

That line is far more flexible today: many couples pool a shared budget from their own savings and contributions from both families, without sticking strictly to "his side, her side." Still, it's worth having the who-pays-for-what conversation with both families early, and writing it down even just for yourselves — it saves you from an awkward moment a month before the wedding when it turns out the tamada was "supposed to be" booked by someone else.

## A financial cushion for the big day

Whatever your budget, build in an 8–10% reserve. It usually goes toward overtime at the venue after midnight (Armenian weddings rarely end on schedule), a guest count that grew at the last minute, and small last-minute adjustments from the decorator or stylist. Couples who plan for this cushion in advance handle these surprises far more calmly than those who budgeted down to the last dram.
